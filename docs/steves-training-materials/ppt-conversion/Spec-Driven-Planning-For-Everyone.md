STEP 1
Gather Before You Write
Before writing a single sentence, go collect what you actually need. Don't draft from memory — draft from evidence.

A
B
C
Scan the Landscape
Go Deep Where It Matters
Resolve Contradictions
Quickly check multiple sources. Read the brief, pull last quarter's report, skim the relevant emails.
Dig into the areas that are unclear or high-stakes. Call the person who knows. Check the actual numbers.
When two sources disagree, settle it now. Don't draft around a question mark — you'll just rewrite later.
"The client brief says Q2 launch, but the contract says Q3. Which is it?"
"Finance confirmed the budget is $40K, not $60K. That changes the whole recommendation."
"Marketing says 12 regions, Operations says 8. Let's get the real number before we scope this."

20 minutes of gathering prevents a full day of rewriting when the facts turn out to be different than you assumed.

STEP 2
Write the Outline, Not the Draft
A strong outline answers six questions. Skip any and you'll discover the gap mid-draft.

1
What's this for and who's it for?
The purpose in one sentence. The audience. What they need from this.

2
What did we learn in gathering?
THE GOLD STANDARD
Key facts, numbers, and anything that surprised you or contradicted assumptions.
Could a colleague pick up this outline and write their section without asking you a single question?

3
What's the recommendation or structure?
The core argument, approach, or framework you're going with — and why.

4
Who writes what?
If yes, it's ready. If they'd need to come find you for clarification, keep refining.
Each section has one owner. No confusion about who's responsible for what.

5
What's the timeline?
Think of it like a recipe: ingredients, steps, timing, and what to watch out for — all in one place.
When are drafts due? When does review happen? When does it go out?

6
How will we know it's done?
Specific criteria. Not "looks good" — real checkpoints you can verify.

THE KEY SKILL
Know What "Done" Looks Like
The most powerful planning skill: write criteria anyone can check — with no debate about whether they're met.

VAGUE CRITERIA
CLEAR CRITERIA

"Make it compelling"
"Opens with the CFO's top 3 concerns"
Compelling to whom? Your idea of compelling isn't mine.
Read page 1. Are the concerns there? Yes or no.

"Clean it up"
"Under 10 pages, 12pt font"
Does that mean formatting? Rewriting? Shortening? All three?
Check page count and font size. Pass or fail in 5 seconds.

"Make sure the numbers work"
"Revenue figures match Q4 actuals"
Which numbers? Against what source? To what precision?
Compare to the source spreadsheet. Numbers match or they don't.

"It should tell a good story"
"Exec summary is 3 bullet points"
This means 10 different things to 10 different reviewers.
Count the bullets. Three? Done. More? Edit.

The Stranger Test
Could someone who wasn't in the meeting check this criterion and say "yes" or "no" in 30 seconds? If yes, it's clear. If they'd need to ask what you meant, rewrite it.

STEP 3
Divide the Work Clearly
Every section needs one owner and a clear definition of done. Shared ownership means nobody owns it.
1
One Owner Per Section

No "we'll both work on it." One name, one section. No confusion about who writes what.
Example: Annual Report
2
Clear "Done" Per Section
Each person knows exactly what their finished section looks like. Length, format, sources cited.
Dana — Financial Summary: 3 pages, uses audited Q4 data. Done when: figures match the audit report exactly.
3
Parallel When Possible
Raj — Market Analysis: 2 pages, cites 4 industry sources. Done when: all claims have a footnote.
Who can work at the same time? Who needs someone else's piece first? Map the dependencies.
Priya — Design and Layout: On-brand template, charts formatted. Done when: passes the brand checklist.

Dana and Raj write in parallel. Priya starts layout once both sections are in.

STEP 4
Get a Fresh Set of Eyes
Before you start writing, have someone else read the outline. It's the cheapest quality check you'll ever do.

You Can't Edit Your Own Blind Spots
REVIEW THE OUTLINE
The person who wrote the outline has the same assumptions baked in. Someone else will see what you can't.
Before writing. Are we covering the right topics? Is the framing right? Did we miss anyone's concerns?

Cheap Now, Expensive Later
REVIEW THE DRAFT
Catching a wrong angle in the outline is a 5-minute fix. Catching it in a finished 15-page report means starting over.
After writing. Does it say what we intended? Are the facts right? Does it flow?

Match Effort to Stakes
FINAL READ-THROUGH
Internal memo? Quick read from a colleague. Board presentation? Multiple reviewers, multiple rounds.
Before sending. Typos, formatting, tone. Last chance to catch something embarrassing.

Getting feedback on an outline is collaboration. Getting feedback after delivery is damage control.

STEP 5
Draft, Review, Revise, Finalize
Write the draft, check it against your criteria, fix what's off. Two revision rounds max — then it's done.

Only small issues?
Note them. Ask the reviewer if they're acceptable.

3

Checklist all green?

It's ready. Send it for final approval.

What needs fixing? What's a known tradeoff? What's fine as-is?

Two revision rounds max. After that, finalize with known tradeoffs or escalate for direction.

Every piece of feedback gets addressed or explicitly accepted as-is. No "I'll get to it later" — that's how things slip through.
