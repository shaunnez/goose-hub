## Opening: AI Is a Conversation, Not a Search

When most of us started using the internet, we learned to Google things. One question, one answer, move on. It's natural to bring that same habit to AI — type a question, get a response, done.

But AI works differently. The first answer is almost never the best answer. It's a **starting point for a conversation.** The people who get the most value from AI treat it like a back-and-forth — they ask, they read, they push, they refine. Just like working through an idea with a colleague.

That's what this session is about. Not how to write the perfect prompt — but how to **think in iterations.** How to work *with* AI through a process, how to judge whether what it gives you is actually good, and how to stay in the driver's seat while you do it.

## Part 1: The Iteration Mindset

### One Turn vs. Multi-Turn Thinking

Here's the difference between how most people use AI and how people who get great results use it:

| One-Turn Thinking | Iteration Thinking |
|---|---|
| "Give me the answer" | "Give me a starting point" |
| Accept or reject the response | Read, react, and refine |
| AI does the work | AI does the drafting, you do the steering |
| One exchange, done | 3-5 exchanges, each one better |
| You evaluate at the end | You evaluate at every step |

Iteration thinking doesn't take much longer. Most iterations are one or two sentences — a quick redirect, a clarification, a "not quite, try this instead." The difference in output quality is enormous.

### You Don't Have to Know Where to Start

One of the biggest barriers to using AI well is feeling like you need to have the perfect question before you begin. You don't.

Some of the best AI conversations start with:

> "I need to put together a presentation for my team about our Q1 results but I'm not sure what angle to take. Can you help me think through what would be most useful for them?"

> "I have a meeting with a frustrated merchant tomorrow. I know the basics of the situation but I'm not sure how to structure the conversation. Can we talk through it?"

> "I keep hearing about a new feature from our competitors and I need to understand what it means for us. I don't even know the right questions to ask yet — can you help me get oriented?"

**The hard part is often getting to the right topic, the right framing, the right question.** You don't have to do that alone. Let AI be your thinking partner for the messy early stage — bouncing ideas around, exploring angles, narrowing down what actually matters.

This is where AI shines. It's endlessly patient, it doesn't judge half-formed thoughts, and it can help you organize scattered ideas into something clear. Use it for the discovery phase, not just the production phase.

**The shift:** Instead of "I need to figure out what I want, *then* go to AI," try "I'll figure out what I want *with* AI." Go on the journey together.

### What Iteration Actually Looks Like

A real example of multi-turn thinking:

**Turn 1 — Explore:**
> "I'm prepping for a meeting with a restaurant owner who's been having payment processing issues during peak hours. What are the main things I should be thinking about?"

**Turn 2 — Narrow:**
> "Good list. The one that matters most here is the business impact during rush. This is a high-volume pizza place — Friday 5-9pm, 80% delivery orders. Let's focus there. Give me 3 talking points."

**Turn 3 — Sharpen:**
> "Those are close but too formal. I'm saying this in person, not reading a slide. Make them conversational."

**Turn 4 — Gut check:**
> "Before I use these — is there anything here you're not confident about? Anything you filled in that I should verify?"

Notice the progression: *explore, narrow, sharpen, verify.* Turn 1 didn't need to be perfect. It was just the starting point. Each turn added judgment, context, and direction. That's iteration.

### Why the First Answer Is Rarely the Best

AI is optimized to give you a *helpful-sounding* response as fast as possible. That means the first answer tends to be:

- Safe and generic (it doesn't know your specifics yet)
- Broad rather than deep (it's hedging because you haven't narrowed the scope)
- Polished-sounding but surface-level

That's not a flaw — it's a starting point. Your job is to make it specific, make it yours, and make it right through a few rounds of conversation.

## Part 2: How to Judge Whether AI Got It Right

Iteration is only useful if you can tell good output from bad output. Here's a practical way to evaluate anything AI gives you.

### The Three-Question Check

Before you use any AI output, run it through these three questions:

**1. "Did it follow my instructions?"** *(10 seconds)*

Quick skim. Right length? Right format? Right audience? If it wrote formal when you asked for casual, or gave you five points when you asked for three — that's a miss. Send it back.

**2. "Is the substance actually right?"** *(60 seconds)*

This is where most mistakes hide. Pick 2-3 specific claims, numbers, or recommendations and ask yourself: *does this match what I know?* If it mentions a statistic, where did it come from? If it makes a recommendation, does the reasoning hold up?

**This is the most important question.** AI is fluent — it writes well-structured, confident-sounding text whether the content is right or wrong. Good writing is not evidence of good thinking. A polished paragraph with a wrong number in it is worse than an ugly paragraph with the right one.

**3. "Did it introduce anything I didn't ask for?"** *(30 seconds)*

Sometimes AI adds things — extra claims, assumptions, context it made up to fill gaps. Scan for anything that feels like it came from the AI rather than from you or your data. If you can't trace a claim back to something you provided, flag it.

**The full check takes under two minutes.** That's worth it for anything you're going to send to someone else, share in a meeting, or act on.

### Red Flags to Watch For

These are signals that the AI may have filled in gaps with guesses rather than telling you it didn't know:

- **Suspiciously specific numbers** you didn't provide ("restaurants typically see a 23% improvement...") — where did that come from?
- **Overly clean answers** with no caveats — real situations have nuance
- **Citations or references** you can't verify — AI sometimes invents sources
- **The answer perfectly matches what you were hoping to hear** — more on this in Part 3

## Part 3: Staying in the Driver's Seat

### The Confirmation Bias Trap

Here's something worth being aware of. It's not a criticism — it's just how these tools work and how our brains work together.

AI is trained to be helpful. In practice, that often means it's trained to be *agreeable.* When you ask "Isn't X a good approach?" it's more likely to say yes than to push back. When you frame a question with an assumption baked in, AI tends to build on that assumption rather than challenge it.

That's fine when you're right. But when you're wrong — or when there's a better option you haven't considered — AI will happily help you build a beautiful case for the wrong answer.

This isn't the AI's fault. It's doing what it's designed to do. And it's not your fault either — we all naturally seek out information that confirms what we already think. It's called **confirmation bias**, and it's one of the most well-documented patterns in human decision-making. AI just makes it easier to fall into, because you now have a very articulate partner who agrees with you almost every time.

### How to Stay Sharp

The antidote isn't suspicion — it's curiosity. A few simple habits:

**Ask AI to challenge you, not just support you:**

> "I'm leaning toward Option A. Before I commit — what's the strongest case for Option B?"

> "What am I not considering here?"

> "If this is wrong, where would it break first?"

**Watch for the "yes-and" pattern.** If AI agrees with everything you say — even when you change direction — it's following you, not advising you. A good advisor pushes back sometimes. If AI never does, you might need to explicitly ask it to.

**Apply the "new colleague" test.** Imagine a smart new team member handed you this same output. Would you send it straight to a client? Or would you read it carefully, check the key points, and maybe adjust the tone? Give AI output the same healthy review you'd give a colleague's first draft.

**Separate "sounds right" from "is right."** AI writes with confidence regardless of accuracy. The more polished and natural the language sounds, the easier it is to trust without verifying. Researchers call this the **fluency trap** — we instinctively treat well-written text as more trustworthy. Being aware of that tendency is most of the battle.

### Know When to Start Fresh

One practical thing: **long AI conversations drift.**

When you use AI to explore a topic — bouncing around ideas, asking follow-ups, changing direction — that's great. That's the discovery phase. But after 15-20 exchanges, the conversation is carrying a lot of history. AI is reading everything you've said, including the dead ends, the wrong turns, and the ideas you abandoned. All of that influences what it says next.

It's like whiteboarding with someone for an hour — eventually the board is so full of crossed-out ideas and arrows that it's hard to see clearly.

**The habit:** Once you've found your direction — you know what you want, the angle is clear, the key points are defined — start a **new conversation** for the final product. Bring your clarity into a clean session. Give AI the refined brief and let it execute without all the exploration baggage.

Think of it as two phases:

1. **Discovery conversation** — messy, exploratory, open-ended. This is where you figure out *what* you want.
2. **Production conversation** — clean, focused, specific. This is where you get the *output* you need.

Separating the two keeps AI sharp and keeps your final product clean.

### The Domain Anchor

Here's the good news: **you are the expert in your domain.** AI doesn't know your merchants, your market, your team dynamics, or the history behind why things are the way they are. You do.

The most reliable way to evaluate AI output is to anchor it against what you already know. When something in an AI response doesn't match your experience, trust your experience first and ask AI to explain the gap. More often than not, your gut was right and AI was filling in blanks.

AI output is only as trustworthy as your ability to evaluate it. In your area of expertise, that ability is high. Lean on it.

## The Five Habits

A quick summary you can take back to your desk:

### 1. Start the Journey Together
You don't need the perfect question. Start with what you have — even if it's "I'm not sure where to begin." Let AI help you find the right direction before you ask it to produce the final thing.

### 2. Iterate, Don't Retype
When the first answer isn't right, don't start over. Tell AI what to fix: "Too formal," "Wrong audience," "That third point doesn't apply here." Each round gets you closer. Three focused iterations beat ten fresh starts.

### 3. Check the Substance, Not Just the Surface
Did it follow instructions? Is the content actually right? Did it add anything it shouldn't have? Two minutes of checking saves you from sending out something that looks great but isn't.

### 4. Ask It to Challenge You
Before you act on an AI recommendation, ask: "What's the other side of this?" It's the simplest way to surface what you might be missing — and to break the confirmation bias loop.

### 5. Fresh Conversation for the Final Product
Use one conversation to explore and discover. Start a new one — with a clear, focused brief — to produce the final output. Clean input, clean output.

## Closing

AI is not an oracle. It's a drafting partner. A very fast, very fluent one that sometimes fills in the blanks without telling you and almost always agrees with what you were already thinking.

The people who get the most from it aren't the ones who write perfect prompts. They're the ones who've built the habit of iterating — reading, reacting, refining. Treating every response as a starting point for a conversation, not a finished answer.

That mindset does two things at once: it makes your AI output better, and it keeps your own expertise sharp. You stay the expert. AI stays the tool.
